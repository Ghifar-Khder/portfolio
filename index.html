Biomedical Electronics

Wearable Pulse Oximeter

This project turns a pulse-oximetry sensor, a compact ESP32 setup, and a small OLED into a wearable monitoring prototype. The goal was to keep the device self-contained: sensing, processing, display, battery power and wireless access all fit into a modified watch-style enclosure.

What it does

The MAX30100 collects the optical pulse signal from the skin. The ESP32 reads the sensor over I²C, processes the available SpO₂ and pulse-rate data, and updates the OLED interface. The same controller also manages the user controls, battery monitoring and wireless connection.

Main hardware

MAX30100 optical pulse-oximetry sensor, ESP32-WROOM-32, 1.3-inch 128×64 SH1106 OLED, 3.7 V lithium battery, boost/charging module, 3.3 V regulation, rotary encoder, push button and a modified watch enclosure.

Build process

The system was first assembled around the ESP32 development board so the sensor, display and controls could be tested separately. After the firmware and interface were stable, the design was reduced in size: the ESP32 module and power electronics were integrated directly, the enclosure was modified to hold the electronics, and the sensor was positioned against the underside of the wrist.

Power & interface

A rechargeable 3.7 V lithium cell powers the device. The power stage generates the required supply rails, while the firmware also monitors battery voltage. The OLED shows SpO₂, BPM, time, battery state and connection status. A rotary encoder and push button are used to move through settings and controls.

Wireless monitoring

The ESP32 can create its own access point or join an existing Wi-Fi network. A small web server exposes the current measurements to a browser and keeps a rolling 24-hour measurement log that can be exported as CSV.

Testing

The completed prototype was compared with a commercial fingertip pulse oximeter across repeated measurements. The readings stayed close to the reference device, while testing also showed that firm and consistent sensor contact with the wrist is important for a stable optical signal.

Prototype developed for engineering and educational work; it is not a certified medical device.
Biomedical Mechanics & Electronics

Automatic CPR Compression Prototype

This project is a non-programmable mechanical/electronic CPR compression prototype. A DC motor drives a rack-and-pinion mechanism to create the linear stroke, while discrete control circuitry reverses the motor at the stroke endpoints and provides selectable compression depth and adjustable cycle rate. The entire design uses no microcontroller.

Prototype operation

Mechanical system

A DC motor is coupled to a pinion that drives a straight rack. This converts rotary motion into the forward-and-return linear movement used for the compression stroke. The mechanism was aligned and tested so the rack could move through the full travel without binding.

Motor direction control

An L298N H-bridge reverses the motor polarity. A CD4017 counter provides the alternating direction states: one state drives the compression stroke and the next drives retraction. The home switch and depth-detection circuit generate the events that change direction.

Depth selection

Copper contact sections are positioned along the moving rack and connected through a resistor network. A carbon brush reads the resulting position voltage. An LM358 comparator and a three-position selector provide the three implemented stroke settings: 4, 5 and 6 cm.

Endpoint & debounce circuit

A limit switch marks the fully retracted home position. Practical testing showed that mechanical switch bounce produced unwanted extra transitions, so an RC low-pass filter and a NE555 monostable pulse shaper were added to produce one clean control pulse per endpoint event.

Power & rate adjustment

The prototype uses two lithium cells in series. An LM7805 supplies the 5 V control electronics, while an XL4015 converter provides an adjustable motor supply. Changing the motor voltage changes the compression rate, which was calibrated separately for the different stroke lengths.

Integration & testing

The actuator and control circuits were first tested separately, then integrated into the wooden enclosure. Repeated test cycles were used to confirm clean reversal at both endpoints, repeatable 4/5/6 cm travel and a target operating range of about 100–120 compressions per minute.

Engineering/educational prototype only. It was tested and calibrated at prototype level; it is not a clinically validated CPR device.
Biomedical Electronics & Optical Sensing

Optical Glucose Measurement Prototype

This project is an experimental optical glucose-estimation prototype built around a modified finger clip. Infrared light is transmitted through the finger, the detected signal is amplified and filtered, and an Arduino applies an experimentally fitted calibration equation to display an estimated glucose value.

What it does

A 940 nm infrared emitter and a PT908 phototransistor are mounted on opposite sides of a modified pulse-oximeter finger clip. The system measures the transmitted optical signal through the finger and converts the conditioned sensor voltage into an estimated glucose value.

Optical & electronic hardware

The prototype uses a 940 nm infrared emitter, PT908 phototransistor, BC327 transistor LED driver, LM358 op-amp, Arduino Uno/ATmega328P controller, LCD, push button and a 5 V supply. The clip fixes the emitter and detector opposite one another and helps reduce ambient-light interference.

Signal conditioning

The phototransistor signal is amplified with an LM358 non-inverting stage at about 2.5× gain. A low-pass filter around 15 Hz follows the amplifier to reduce electrical noise and pulse-related fluctuations before the Arduino reads the analog signal.

Calibration

Paired measurements were collected using the prototype sensor voltage and a conventional invasive home glucometer. The data were fitted in Excel to obtain a project-specific calibration equation, which was then implemented in the Arduino firmware.

Interface & measurement modes

The firmware supports a single measurement from a short button press and a continuous mode from a long press. The LCD shows the calculated estimate and explicit error messages for invalid use, including missing or badly positioned fingers.

Prototype testing

The completed sensor, amplifier, controller and display were integrated into a portable prototype. Experimental estimates were compared with readings from a conventional home glucometer. This was prototype-level testing rather than clinical validation.

Experimental engineering prototype only. It is not a certified glucose meter and must not be used for diagnosis, treatment or insulin-dosing decisions.
